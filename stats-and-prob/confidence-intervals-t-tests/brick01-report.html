Concept 1: The confidence interval

The intuition. We measured 5,178 people and got an average BMI of 29.66. But that's just our sample — the true average across all US adults is some nearby number we can't see directly. A 95% confidence interval is a net we cast around our sample average: (29.46, 29.86). Read it as: "we're 95% confident the true average BMI is in here."

How it's built. Take the sample average, then go out a few standard errors in each direction. The standard error is the wiggle in our average: s / √n. More people → smaller wiggle → tighter net. The "few" is 1.96-ish (from the t-distribution; with 5,178 people it's basically 1.96).

What it is not. It is not "95% of people are in this range" — it's about the average, not individuals. And 95% confidence is a property of the method, not a probability about this specific interval.

Prove it. Take five BMIs — 22.1, 24.3, 21.8, 25.0, 23.2 — and do the arithmetic by hand: average 23.28, standard error, times 2.776 from a t-table. You get (21.57, 24.99). My mean_ci function gives (21.57, 24.99). scipy gives (21.57, 24.99). Three independent paths, same net. The machinery is sound.

Concept 2: The two-tailed t-test

The question. Do men and women have different average BMIs? Not "who's higher" — just different. That's what two-tailed means: we test for a difference in either direction.

The setup. H0 (the null): the true averages are equal. H1: they differ. The t-statistic is signal over noise: t = (mean_men − mean_women) / SE_of_the_difference. Big t means the gap dwarfs the wiggle. The p-value then asks: if H0 were true, how often would we see a |t| this big by pure luck?

The run. two_sample_ttest(men_bmi, women_bmi):

  • Difference (men − women): −0.82 BMI points, 95% CI (−1.21, −0.42)
  • t = −4.03, p = 0.0001 → reject H0. The averages differ.
  • Cohen's d = −0.11 → the difference is tiny.

The lesson inside the lesson. p = 0.0001 screams "significant!" while d = −0.11 whispers "but barely." With 5,178 people, even a trivial gap clears the significance bar. Always report the effect size next to the p-value. Significance answers "is there anything?"; the effect size answers "should I care?"

Prove it. (1) I generated two fake groups where I planted a true difference of exactly 5. The test recovered 5.11 with p ≈ 0 — it finds what I hid. (2) I generated two fake groups with no difference planted: p = 0.64, stays quiet — it doesn't cry wolf. (3) On the real BMI data, my function matches scipy's ttest_ind to the last decimal. The method passed its exam before touching real data.

The checklist (what we're checking for, every time)

  1. State the question, H0, and whether it's two-tailed.
  2. Check the basics: independent observations, n large enough (or roughly symmetric data).
  3. Compute: difference, t, p — plus the confidence interval of the difference.
  4. Compute the effect size (Cohen's d).
  5. Conclude in one plain-English sentence with both numbers: "yes it differs (p = …), but the gap is tiny (d = …)."
Your turn. Run the same recipe on sbp by sex: two_sample_ttest(men_sbp, women_sbp). Is the difference significant? Should you care?
