Part I — Linear regression

Fitting OLS in Python: the 60-second version

A linear regression says: outcome = intercept + slope × predictor + …, and ordinary least squares (OLS) picks the numbers that make the squared prediction errors as small as possible. The running example: predicting the cost of a hospital stay for 120 patients from age and length of stay (numeric) and admission type (categorical).

The fitted model, in words: every extra year of age adds about $51 to the bill; every extra day adds about $954. Admission type enters as comparisons against the reference category (Elective, whose effect is folded into the intercept): an otherwise identical Emergency patient costs about $3,055 more, an Urgent patient about $1,242 more. Model R² = 0.72 — it explains about 0.72 of the variation in cost, as a proportion. And the point of the exercise — a prediction for a new patient, 68 years old, Emergency admission, 5-day stay: $15,247.

Data structure + Python code

Data: one row per patient; the outcome in one column, predictors in the others. Categorical predictors go in as text — C() in the formula tells statsmodels to dummy-code them against a reference level. Predicting needs a small DataFrame with the same columns.

import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.formula.api import ols

# hosp: 120 rows; columns cost, age, los_days, admission (Elective/Emergency/Urgent)
fit = ols("cost ~ age + los_days + C(admission)", data=hosp).fit()
print(fit.summary())        # coefficients, R-squared = 0.72, p-values

new_patient = pd.DataFrame({"age": [68], "los_days": [5], "admission": ["Emergency"]})
fit.predict(new_patient)    # -> $15,247

Transformations: making it linear, and back-transforming to interpret

OLS fits straight lines in the variables you give it — nothing says you must hand it the raw variables. When a relationship bends, transform a variable until the bend straightens, fit the line there, then back-transform the answer to say it in original units. Example: drug dose vs symptom reduction, where each doubling of the dose buys roughly the same benefit (diminishing returns). Try it:

How you know which one you need: look at the scatter (and later, at the residual plot). A bend that flattens out is asking you to compress the big end of a variable — log does it strongly, square root gently. A bend that accelerates upward asks for the opposite — stretch it, with a square. And a fan-shaped residual plot (errors growing with the prediction) is the same story told about the outcome: compress y.

TransformationWhat it doesReach for it whenReading the result

sqrt(y)A gentler log(y) Mild skew; counts-like outcomes where spread grows with the level Square the predictions to get back to original units
1/y (inverse)The strongest tail-pull of the three Very strong skew; amounts that are really times or rates underneath Signs flip — safest to back-transform predictions (take the reciprocal) and read those

x² (fit x and x²)Lets one predictor bend once: a U or an upside-down U Risk or response is best in the middle — BMI vs risk is the classic shape No single slope exists; plot the curve and quote values off it

Back-transformation, the part everyone forgets: a model fitted to log(cost) predicts log-dollars. Exponentiate the prediction to get dollars back. Two consequences: the coefficient reads as a percentage change rather than a dollar change, and the exponentiated prediction lands near the typical (median) patient rather than the mean — a feature for skewed costs, as long as you know it's happening.

Fit metrics: one family, five summaries

Every metric in this section is a summary of the same ingredients: the residuals (actual − predicted, one per patient). Square them and add them up and you get the sum of squared errors — for the cost model, SSE ≈ 295 million squared-dollars. Useless on its own, but everything else is a rescaling of it:

MetricFormula in wordsCost modelUse it when
RMSESquare root of (SSE / n)$1,567 The default "how wrong are we, typically?" — in the outcome's own units. Honest version: computed on new data; on the training data it's optimistic.
Residual standard error (RSE) Square root of (SSE / (n − predictors − 1))$1,601 RMSE's training-room cousin. Dividing by a smaller number charges the model for each predictor it used, so adding junk predictors can't fake an improvement. This is the number R-style output reports.
MAEAverage of |residual|$1,226 When a few huge errors shouldn't dominate the verdict. Diagnostic pairing: if RMSE is much bigger than MAE, a handful of very large misses are running the show.
MAPEAverage of |residual / actual|, as a %10% When the audience thinks in percentages, or you're comparing across outcomes with different scales. Breaks down when actuals sit near zero — a $50 error on a $60 bill is 83%.
R²1 − SSE / (total variation in y)0.72 "How much of the variation did we explain?" — a proportion, good for communicating, silent about error size in real units. Adjusted R² (0.71 here) applies the same per-predictor charge as RSE; use it when comparing models with different numbers of predictors.

So they're not competing metrics — they're siblings. Square the residuals and you get the RMSE/RSE line; take absolute values instead and you get MAE; divide by the actuals first and you get MAPE; compare SSE against the outcome's total variation and you get R².

Python code: the whole family from one fit
import numpy as np

resid = fit.resid                       # actual - predicted, one per patient
n = len(resid)
k = 4                                   # slopes: age, los_days, Emergency, Urgent
sse = float((resid ** 2).sum())
rmse = np.sqrt(sse / n)                 # $1,567
rse  = np.sqrt(sse / (n - k - 1))       # $1,601
mae  = np.abs(resid).mean()             # $1,226
mape = (np.abs(resid) / hosp["cost"]).mean() * 100   # 10%
print(fit.rsquared, fit.rsquared_adj)   # 0.72, 0.71

Diagnostic plots: what you're looking for, and why

Three standard plots, all computed from the cost model's residuals. This model is well-behaved, so these show what "fine" looks like — each caption names the trouble pattern it's built to catch.

Residuals vs fitted. The residuals should form a formless cloud around zero at every fitted level. A curve in the cloud means a relationship you didn't model — a bend asking for a transformation or an extra term. A funnel (spread widening left to right) means your errors grow with the size of the prediction.

Normal Q-Q plot. Standardized residuals plotted against where they'd sit if they were perfectly normal. On the diagonal line = normal enough. This matters for confidence and prediction intervals and for small samples; big samples and point predictions forgive a lot. Tails that peel away at both ends mean heavier tails than normal — usually a few extreme patients.

Scale-location plot. The square root of the absolute standardized residuals against fitted values — the funnel detector with the direction stripped out, so spread is all you see. A flat band means constant error variance (homoscedasticity). A rising trend is the funnel again: bigger predictions, bigger misses — often the cue for log(y).

Outliers, leverage, and influence: three different accusations

These three words get used interchangeably and mean different things. A small clinic dataset (age → cost of stay, 30 patients) with one of each planted in it:

Outlier — a point that doesn't fit the pattern: a big residual. The 52-year-old whose stay cost $12,500 against a predicted ~$6,100 (standardized residual 5.9). Being an outlier is about y disagreeing with the model.

Leverage — a point whose x is unusual: far from the crowd horizontally, so the line has to pass near it, like the long end of a lever. The 96-year-old has the most leverage here (hat value 0.21, vs a typical ~0.07). But he sits right on the trend, so he barely moves anything — leverage is only potential influence.

Influence — leverage × disagreement: a point that actually bends the fit. The 94-year-old with a $2,600 stay has extreme age and sits far off the line; delete him and the slope visibly changes. Cook's distance measures exactly this — one number per point: how much the whole fit would move if that point were removed. Rules of thumb: look closer above 4/n (here 0.13); above 1 (his is 1.33), definitely investigate. Investigating means: check for a data error first, then report the fit with and without the point. It does not mean silently deleting inconvenient patients.

Python code: hat values and Cook's distance
from statsmodels.formula.api import ols

fit2 = ols("cost ~ age", data=clinic).fit()
influence = fit2.get_influence()
hats = influence.hat_matrix_diag        # leverage, one per patient
cooks = influence.cooks_distance[0]     # Cook's distance, one per patient
# flag for a closer look: cooks > 4 / len(clinic)

Planted results: the influential patient's Cook's distance is 1.33 — the largest in the dataset. The on-trend 96-year-old's is 0.08 despite his leverage.

Part II — Logistic regression

Read this first: logistic regression is linear regression's sibling, not a different species. If the machinery above — fit a line, read coefficients, grade the fit — is new to you, work through Part I first; everything here builds on it.

Why logistic? The failure of the straight line

New problem: predict whether a patient is readmitted within 30 days (yes/no) from a risk score, for 240 patients (42% were actually readmitted). Fit a straight line to a 0/1 outcome anyway and two things break. The line escapes: it predicts probabilities of 1.2 and −0.1, which don't exist. And it insists the effect of 10 extra risk points is identical everywhere — but probability is squeezed into [0, 1], so real risk has to bend: slow at the bottom, fast in the middle, slow at the top.

The logistic fix is one move: don't put the line on the probability — put it on the log-odds, then squash the result back through the S-curve so every prediction lands inside [0, 1]. The fitted line here is log-odds = -2.62 + 0.046 × risk score. What log-odds, odds, and probability each mean — and why you'd ever want all three — is the next section.

Data structure + Python code

Data: one row per patient; the outcome is 0/1, the predictor is a number. Same formula shape as OLS — logit instead of ols.

from statsmodels.formula.api import logit

# rd: 240 rows; columns score (0-100), readmit (0/1)
lfit = logit("readmit ~ score", data=rd).fit()
print(lfit.summary())       # log-odds = -2.62 + 0.046 * score

Why each column earns its place:

x — the input. What you know about the patient before deciding anything.

y — what actually happened. The truth you grade against. Note the patient with probability 0.35 who was readmitted: a probability is not a promise, and a "wrong" prediction at 0.35 isn't necessarily a model failure.

Probability — the human language. "About a 1-in-3 chance of coming back." This is the only column you'd say out loud to a clinician, an executive, or a patient.

Predicted response — probability forced into an action. A threshold (here the default 0.5) turns "how likely" into "flag or don't flag." The threshold is not part of the model — it's a decision about which mistake is cheaper, and the next section is about choosing it.

Odds = probability / (1 − probability) — the betting language. Effects multiply on this scale, which is why papers report odds ratios: in this model, every +10 risk points multiplies the odds of readmission by 1.6×, wherever you start from. (Careful: an odds ratio is not a probability ratio — at high base rates they drift apart.)

Log-odds — the model's native language. This is the straight line actually being fitted (-2.62 + 0.046 × score): add a predictor, add a term; effects are additive here. Nobody quotes log-odds to a human — but every other column is a conversion of it, so it's the one to compute in.

Python code: getting all five from the fit
import numpy as np

patients = pd.DataFrame({"score": [20, 40, 55, 70, 85]})   # the five patients in the table
prob = lfit.predict(patients)                    # probabilities, straight from the fit
log_odds = np.log(prob / (1 - prob))             # invert the squash: back to the line
odds = prob / (1 - prob)
pred = (prob >= 0.5).astype(int)                 # the 0.5 threshold, made explicit

predict() hands back probabilities. The log-odds is one algebra step away — log(p / (1 − p)) — because that formula and the S-curve are exact inverses.

The five standard metrics, in words (tap one above to see which boxes it uses):

Accuracy = (TP + TN) / everyone — "overall, how often are we right?" At the 0.50 threshold: 74%. Handle with care: with a rare outcome, a model that flags nobody also scores high accuracy while being useless.

Sensitivity (recall) = TP / (TP + FN) — of the patients who truly were readmitted, how many did we flag? At 0.50: 66%. This is the "don't miss cases" metric.

Specificity = TN / (TN + FP) — of the patients who truly stayed home, how many did we leave alone? At 0.50: 79%.

Precision = TP / (TP + FP) — of the patients we flagged, how many truly were readmitted? At 0.50: 70%. This is the "don't cry wolf" metric — it prices your follow-up team's time.

F1 = the harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity — one number for when both kinds of mistake matter and you refuse to pick a favorite. At 0.50: 68%.

The threshold is a policy decision, not a statistic. Drag it down and you catch more readmissions (sensitivity climbs) but flag more healthy patients (precision falls, follow-up costs climb). Drag it up and the reverse. There is no mathematically best threshold — only the one that matches what a missed readmission costs you versus what a false alarm costs you.
Python code: the matrix at threshold 0.50
probs = lfit.predict(rd)                               # one probability per patient
pred = (probs >= 0.5).astype(int)                      # threshold, made explicit
tp = int(((pred == 1) & (rd["readmit"] == 1)).sum())   # 67
fp = int(((pred == 1) & (rd["readmit"] == 0)).sum())   # 29
tn = int(((pred == 0) & (rd["readmit"] == 0)).sum())   # 110
fn = int(((pred == 0) & (rd["readmit"] == 1)).sum())   # 34
