Phase 1 Report: Which blood biomarkers actually associate with hypertension?

Bio-ML Library · Phase 1 · NHANES 2017–2018 (CDC, public domain) · n = 5,437 adults

The question

Across nine blood biomarkers measured in a real national health survey, which ones are genuinely associated with having hypertension — once we correct for the fact that we tested nine things at once?

How I went about it

  1. Download. Pulled ten public files from the CDC: demographics, blood-pressure exam and questionnaire, cholesterol labs, HbA1c, glucose, CRP, and body measures.
  2. Clean and merge. Joined everything on the respondent ID, averaged the repeated blood-pressure readings, recoded questionnaire answers, and mapped lab columns to clean names. Real-world mess, handled explicitly in code.
  3. Define the outcome. Hypertension = mean systolic ≥ 130, or mean diastolic ≥ 80, or currently on blood-pressure medication (the ACC/AHA-style rule).
  4. Compare groups. For each biomarker: survey-weighted means, a weighted two-sample t-test, a Mann-Whitney robustness check, and an effect size.
  5. Correct for nine tests. Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg.
  6. Interrogate confounding. Age/sex-adjusted logistic regression plus age-stratified means: is it the biomarker, or just age?
  7. Extract the bricks. Every reusable piece went into the toolkit/ code bank for future phases.

The walkthrough: five lessons from this phase

Lesson 1 — What a p-value actually is.
Plain English: a p-value is the probability of seeing a difference at least this big if there were really no difference at all. Small p = "this would be a weird coincidence." It is not the probability that the effect is real, and it says nothing about how big the effect is.
Formal: p = P(data this extreme | H0), where H0 is "equal means." Our test statistic was Welch's t on survey-weighted means.
Lesson 2 — Nine tests, nine chances to fool yourself.
Plain English: if you test 20 useless biomarkers at the 5% level, you'll "discover" about one fake association on average. That's not fraud, it's arithmetic. Bonferroni fixes it by demanding p < 0.05/9 — strict, sometimes too strict. Benjamini–Hochberg instead controls the false discovery rate: among the things you call significant, at most 5% are false alarms. Watch HDL in our table: raw p = 0.007 looks exciting, Bonferroni kills it, FDR keeps it. Same data, three verdicts — the method is the message.
Formal: Bonferroni controls the family-wise error rate at α; BH controls E[V/R] ≤ α. With m = 9 tests, Bonferroni's bar is p < 0.0056.
Lesson 3 — Why the survey weights exist.
Plain English: NHANES deliberately oversamples some groups (older adults, some minorities) so it can study them precisely. A plain head-count would then misrepresent America. Every respondent carries a weight saying "I stand for this many people." Our 48% prevalence number uses those weights; the unweighted number would be wrong in a boring, silent way — the most dangerous kind.
Formal: we used the MEC exam weight WTMEC2YR; weighted means and a Welch-style t with Kish effective sample sizes. Full design-based standard errors would also use the PSU/strata variables — noted as a refinement.
Lesson 4 — Effect size vs. significance.
Plain English: with 5,000 people, even a trivial difference can be "highly significant." The p-value answers "is there anything?" Cohen's d answers "how much?" Waist circumference doesn't just have the smallest p — it has the biggest d (0.67, a medium-to-large gap). Always report both; never let a tiny p-value sell you a tiny effect.
Formal: d = (μ₁ − μ₂)/s_pooled on weighted moments; 0.2/0.5/0.8 are the conventional small/medium/large benchmarks.
Lesson 5 — Confounding: is it the biomarker, or just age?
Plain English: older people have higher blood pressure and worse biomarkers. So a biomarker can "predict" hypertension while merely being a proxy for age. We checked two ways: a regression that holds age and sex fixed, and Figure 3, which compares groups within age bands. The metabolic signals survive both. And the fun one: LDL cholesterol shows no signal at all — plausibly because people with high LDL take statins, which lowers their LDL while they stay older and hypertensive. A null result can be the most informative one in the room, and treatment effects poisoning observational comparisons is exactly what phase 2 (causal inference) is for.
Formal: per-biomarker logistic regression, logit(P) = β₀ + β₁·z_biomarker + β₂·age + β₃·male, survey-weighted; biomarkers standardized so odds ratios are per-SD and comparable.

Limitations (honestly)

  • Cross-sectional: association, not causation. Nothing here says what causes hypertension.
  • Weighted t-tests are approximations; full survey-design standard errors are a refinement.
  • Fasting analytes ideally want fasting-subsample weights; the MEC weight was used and documented.
  • Medication use (statins especially) contaminates the lipid comparisons — see the LDL story.

What's next

Phase 2: causal inference. We take the confounding lesson seriously — propensity methods on NHANES to estimate effects rather than associations, then possibly CMS synthetic claims data, which looks like the real-world data of your day job.
