The Scenario

Every test begins with a real-world problem. For example, imagine we are testing a new product feature to determine if it should be rolled out to all users. Currently, an online self-service help search bar is located at the bottom of our application. However, our in-person customer support by phone is overwhelmed, and we therefore want to increase self-service usage.

  • Our Hypothesis: Moving the search bar to the top of the page will increase self-service platform usage, consequently decreasing customer support call volume.
  • Primary Metric (what we care about):
    • The conversion rate of the self-service platform, defined as: (Total number of users who used the online self-service platform / Total number of application visitors within a specified time frame).
  • Guardrail Metrics (what we want to make sure doesn’t change):
    • Note: We would also implement guardrail metrics to ensure other aspects of the business don’t negatively change, but we will focus on the primary metric here.

To make our example concrete, let’s use this scenario: We currently get 100 visitors a week, and 2 of them use the help platform. Therefore, our status-quo baseline is 2%. We want to increase that rate to 5%. For practical business reasons, we will not ship the product change unless we are confident it results in at least that 5% usage rate.

To test this, we will expose a random group of users to Control A (the status quo) and another random group to Treatment B (the new placement) to see if we can gather enough evidence that the product change works. Note: we randomize the users based on user_id.

Experiment Design & Parameters

Now that we understand the problem, we must lock in our statistical parameters before launching the experiment. Defining these upfront prevents us from moving the goalposts later:

  • Historical Baseline (p₁): The current status quo of the primary metric (2%).
  • Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE): The minimum change we want to observe to justify shipping the feature (5% - 2% = +3%).
  • Alpha / Significance Level (α): Usually set at 5% (0.05). (That is to say: the p-value is the probability of observing our results if there was no real difference. If we observe a p-value less than 5%, that is a small enough probability to reject the null hypothesis. We’d accept a 5% risk of sounding a false alarm (Type I Error).)
  • Power (1 - β): The probability of correctly detecting a true effect (rejecting the null hypothesis when there is a true difference at least the size of our MDE). Usually set at 80%.
  • Beta (β): The Type II Error rate (1 - Power = 0.20). The risk of failing to sound the alarm when there actually was a real difference.
  • Group Split: The distribution of traffic between Control A and Treatment B. We’ll use a 50/50 split.

Standardizing Effect Size: Cohen’s h

Going back to our scenario where we determined that the minimum detectable effect was +3% (that came from a 2% baseline to a 5% minimum proportion for the feature change to ship). Now imagine if we had another scenario where our current baseline was 95% and we wanted the new proportion to be 98%. In that scenario, that is also a 3-point difference in Minimum Detectable Effect. However, moving from 2% to 5% is harder than moving from 95% to 98% because variance changes at the extreme ends of a percentage scale.

Data Interpretation and Decision

Let’s say you launch the experiment, and now it’s been 12 weeks. After running the experiment and gathering our 1,176 data points, our dataset looks like a ledger of binary outcomes:

  • User 1 | Control | Did not use (0)
  • User 2 | Treatment | Used (1)

To determine if there is a statistically significant difference between the two proportions, we run a Two-Sample Proportion Z-Test. If the resulting p-value is less than our alpha (0.05), we reject the null hypothesis and confidently launch the feature.

Note: For more information on hypothesis testing, especially when sample sizes are small and/or the underlying data is not normally distributed (thereby requiring non-parametric tests), check out my deeper guide on Hypothesis Testing here.

Pitfalls and Corrections

In a perfect world, a single Z-test is all you need. However, real-world realities often complicate our clean experimental design.

1. Family-Wise Error Rates (Multiple Variants)

What happens if we test multiple treatments at once?

For example, we have Control A and Treatment B currently. But what if we also have Treatment C and Treatment D, which are other variants of treatment B. Let’s say Treatment B moves the search bar to the top of the page. Treatment C might have the search bar at the bottom of the page but in big bold letters. Treatment D might have the search bar at the bottom of the page but in red text.

If we tested each variant independently, each variant would carry a 5% chance of a Type I error (sounding a false alarm), as designed above. 2. Multiple Metrics

Similarly, if you track your primary metric alongside 20 secondary metrics (e.g., sign-up rates, click-through rates, average purchase size, etc.), there is a ~64% chance that at least one of those secondary metrics will show a statistically significant difference purely by chance.

The Fix:

  • Establish a single primary metric before the test begins and make your business decision solely on that.
  • If you must make decisions based on multiple metrics, apply a correction (like Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg) to lower your p-value threshold, protecting you from false discoveries.

Sanity Checks and Post-Test Diagnostics

Even if your p-value screams “success,” you cannot trust it until you verify that the experiment’s underlying mechanics and user behaviors didn’t artificially skew the data.

Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM) & A/A Tests

If we design a 50/50 split, we won’t get an exact 50.0% / 50.0% distribution due to random noise—we might observe a 52/48 split. To verify whether this is harmless noise or a broken randomization pipeline, we run a Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test comparing our observed traffic split against our expected 50/50 split.

  • The Action: If the Chi-Square test returns a statistically significant p-value (typically evaluated at a strict threshold like p < 0.001), we have a Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM). This means a bug (e.g., logging failures, redirects, or slow page loads in Treatment B) is selectively dropping users. When SRM occurs, the test is invalid: stop the experiment, fix the engineering bug, and restart.
  • Proactive Prevention (A/A Testing): Before launching a major feature, run an A/A test where both Control and Treatment receive the exact same status quo experience. If your randomization engine and telemetry are healthy, you should observe no SRM and no statistically significant difference in conversion rates (beyond the expected 5% false-positive rate at α = 0.05).

Covariate Imbalance & Simpson’s Paradox

Different user segments naturally behave differently. In our scenario, mobile users might, for example, use the self-service search bar at a much higher baseline rate than desktop users. If our randomization is unbalanced across these segments, or if we change traffic allocation percentages mid-test (ramping up Treatment B from 10% to 50% while weekend mobile traffic spikes), we might trigger Simpson’s Paradox.

  • The Problem: Simpson’s Paradox occurs when a trend appears in separate subgroups (e.g., Treatment B wins on Mobile and wins on Desktop individually) but reverses or disappears when the data is aggregated together because one group’s disproportionate volume weights the overall average.
  • The Fix: Use stratified sampling (blocking) during experiment design to guarantee an equal 50/50 split within both Mobile and Desktop cohorts, and always evaluate treatment lift across key segments alongside the aggregate topline metric.

Primacy (Change Aversion) & Novelty Effects

Existing users have muscle memory, which can temporarily distort experiment results in two opposite ways:

  • Novelty Effect: Returning users click the new top search bar simply because it is shiny and new, creating an artificial early spike in conversion that fades over time.
  • Change Aversion (Primacy Effect): Returning users find the change jarring; they habitually scroll to the bottom of the page looking for the old search bar, causing an initial dip in usage before they adapt to the new layout.

How to Diagnose and Fix:

  1. Plot Treatment Lift Over Time: Track the daily difference between Treatment and Control. If the lift starts unusually high and decays (Novelty) or starts low and climbs (Change Aversion) before flattening out, you have a time-based bias. You may need to run the test longer until user behavior stabilizes.
  2. Segment by New vs. Returning Users: This is the cleanest analytical check. Because New Users have zero prior experience with the old user interface, they are immune to both novelty and change aversion. Comparing Treatment vs. Control strictly among new users isolates the true, unconditioned impact of the feature.

Ratio Metrics and the Delta Method

Beyond standard metrics, you might encounter metrics that don’t fit perfectly into our straightforward Z-test structure. To understand Ratio Metrics and the Delta Method, we first have to recognize that every A/B test has a Unit of Randomization (how we split traffic, which is almost always by User) and a Unit of Analysis (what we are actually counting in the denominator of our metric).

Everything is easy when those two units match. Ratio metrics—and the headache that requires the Delta Method—happen when those two units do not match.

The Setup: Standard Metric vs. Ratio Metric in Our Playbook

In our playbook’s current example, we are randomized at the User level (Control A vs. Treatment B).

  • Our Current Metric (Simple Proportion): Total Users Who Used Search / Total Users Who Visited

Here, the denominator is Users. Because we randomized by User and our denominator is Users, every single row in our dataset is 100% independent. User 1’s behavior has zero impact on User 2’s behavior. Our normal Z-test and standard error formula work out of the box.

Now, let’s introduce a Ratio Metric to our scenario:

Suppose we say, “Looking at whether a user used the search bar once isn’t enough. Some users log in 10 times a week. We want to know if moving the search bar to the top of the page increases the rate at which the search bar is used per session (or per pageview).”

  • The New Metric (Ratio Metric): Total Search Bar Uses / Total Application Sessions
  • We still have to randomize by User (because it would be a terrible user experience if the search bar jumped from the bottom to the top of the screen every time the same person logged in).
  • The Catch: We are randomizing by User, but our metric’s denominator is now Sessions.

Other classic business examples of ratio metrics include:

  • Click-Through Rate (CTR) per Pageview: Total Clicks / Total Pageviews (Randomized by User)
  • Revenue per Order (Average Order Value): Total Revenue / Total Orders (Randomized by User)
  • Support Calls per Help Article Read: Total Support Calls / Total Articles Viewed (Randomized by User)

The Problem: Why Does This Break Our Normal Math?

Remember how our Standard Error formula () is the bedrock of our confidence intervals and p-values? (See Appendix for more information). That formula relies on one strict mathematical rule: every observation in the denominator (N) must be completely independent.

When your denominator is Sessions instead of Users, you violate that rule in two ways:

  1. Intra-User Correlation (Clusters of Data): Suppose you have 588 users in Treatment B, and together they generate 5,000 sessions. You cannot plug n = 5,000 into your standard error formula! Why? Because 50 of those sessions might belong to “Power User Bob,” who loves the search bar and uses it every single time. Those 50 sessions are not 50 independent data points; they are heavily correlated because they all came from one guy. If you pretend n = 5,000, your standard error will be artificially tiny, your confidence intervals will be way too narrow, and you will get massive false positives (Type I errors).
  2. Two Random Variables Moving at Once: In a normal user-level test, the denominator (N = 588 users) is fixed by you, and only the numerator (how many users click) is a random variable. But in a ratio metric, both the numerator and the denominator are random variables at the user level. Moving the search bar to the top of the page might change how many searches a user does (numerator), and it might accidentally change how many sessions a user logs (denominator)—maybe they solve their problem faster and don’t need to log back in.

Because you are dividing one random, noisy user-level number by another random, noisy user-level number, the normal variance formula completely falls apart.

The Solution: Use the Delta Method

So, how do we calculate the variance (and Standard Error) when our metric is a ratio of two random user-level variables (User Searches / User Sessions)? Enter the Delta Method.

Without getting bogged down in calculus, here is the pure intuition of what the Delta Method does:

  • It keeps the analysis at the User level: Instead of looking at a giant pool of 5,000 sessions, the Delta Method looks at the 588 independent users. For each user, it records two numbers: Yᵢ (how many searches they did) and Xᵢ (how many sessions they had).
  • It accounts for three things simultaneously: Because dividing two random variables creates a weird, non-linear shape, the Delta Method uses a mathematical trick (a first-order Taylor series approximation) to “flatten” that relationship into a straight line so we can estimate the variance. To do this, it combines:
    • The variance of the numerator (How much do searches vary from user to user?)
    • The variance of the denominator (How much do session counts vary from user to user?)
    • The covariance between the two (Do users who have more sessions also naturally do more searches?)

The Business Intuition of the Result:

When you run your data through the Delta Method formula, it spits out a corrected, honest Standard Error.

Because it recognizes that 5,000 sessions came from only 588 independent users (and adjusts for heavy power users via the covariance), the Delta Method gives you a wider, more realistic confidence interval.

Once the Delta Method gives you that corrected Standard Error, you go right back to your normal playbook: you plug that new Standard Error into your Z-test or Confidence Interval () and make your business decision safely.

Beyond the Basics: Advanced Topics & Future Directions

Once you master standard A/B testing, you will inevitably run into real-world business constraints (e.g., limited engineering bandwidth, overlapping experiments, small traffic volumes, or interconnected user bases) that require specialized methodologies:

  • Painted Door Tests (Fake Door Tests): A lightweight product validation technique where you present users with a button or UI element for a feature that hasn’t actually been built yet to gauge organic demand before investing engineering resources. In our playbook’s scenario, if the self-service help platform didn’t exist yet, we could place a “Search Help Articles” bar at the top of the page; when a user clicks it, a modal pops up saying, “We’re still building this! In the meantime, call support at...” Tracking the click rate validates whether users even want self-service before we write a single line of backend search code.
  • Multifactorial Design & Interaction Effects: Instead of testing one change at a time, multifactorial (or multivariate) testing evaluates multiple distinct variables simultaneously to see how they perform both individually and together. For example, if we want to test Search Bar Placement (Bottom vs. Top) and Button Copy (“Help” vs. “Ask a Question”) at the same time, a 2 x 2 factorial design splits traffic into four cohorts. This allows us to detect interaction effects—such as discovering that changing the copy has zero impact at the bottom of the page, but creates a massive lift when paired with the top placement.
  • Frequentist vs. Bayesian A/B Testing: Everything in this playbook relies on Frequentist statistics, where the true conversion rate is treated as a fixed, unknown number, and we need a pre-calculated sample size (1,176 users) to compute p-values and confidence intervals. Bayesian A/B testing flips this paradigm: it blends prior historical knowledge (“priors”) with incoming live test data to update a probability distribution of the true conversion rate. In business, Bayesian testing is popular when traffic is low or when stakeholders want intuitive, real-time answers—allowing you to legally say, “There is an 88% probability that Treatment B is better than Control A” without falling into the frequentist semantic trap.
  • SUTVA Violations & Network Effects (Interference): Standard A/B tests rely on the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA), which strictly requires that one user’s assignment to Treatment B has zero impact on the behavior or experience of a user in Control A. This assumption breaks down in social networks, two-sided marketplaces (like Uber or Airbnb), or collaborative B2B apps due to network effects (or spillover). In our playbook example, if a user in Treatment B discovers the new top search bar and slacks a direct link to the self-service portal to their coworker who happens to be in Control A, Control A’s usage rate goes up artificially. This “contaminates” the control group, masks the true treatment effect, and usually requires advanced cluster randomization (e.g., randomizing by entire company, geography, or time window rather than individual user).

The Central Limit Theorem in Practice

In academia, the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is often taught as an exercise: if you take infinite samples (B = ∞) of size n from a population, the distribution of those sample means will form a normal curve, called the sampling distribution. This sampling distribution has a mean and standard deviation. The mean of the sampling distribution is the population mean, and the standard deviation of the sampling distribution is called the standard error (SE). The Standard Error is important because that lets us determine confidence intervals, or the range in which we can be confident in our estimation of the population mean.

This academic exercise is all to demonstrate that the standard error is inversely proportional to the square root of N—as sample size goes up, standard error goes down. (Intuition: the bigger the sample size, the extreme values get averaged out more, placing the sample mean closer to the true population mean, and therefore there is less wobble around the true population mean when building a sampling distribution).

You might ask: doesn’t SE also rely on the variance of the sample (p̂)*(1-p̂); and if we took another sample, the variance of that sample would be different; and therefore the SE and confidence would be different? Yes, that is true. However:

  • In the theoretical world, when you take infinite samples and plot their proportions on a histogram, the mean of that entire sampling distribution is actually the true population proportion, p. Each individual sample generates its own p̂, and if you averaged all of those infinite p̂s together, you would land perfectly on p.
  • In the real world, you only pull one sample, which gives you exactly one p̂. Because your n is large enough, the math guarantees that your single p̂ will fall extremely close to the center of that theoretical bell curve. Therefore, we confidently use that single p̂ as a highly accurate, practical estimate of the invisible population parameter (proportion) p.

The “95% Confidence” Semantic Trap

When we calculate a 95% confidence interval (e.g., [3.77%, 6.23%]) from our single A/B test, it is tempting to say, “There is a 95% chance the true conversion rate is in this specific range.” Technically, this is false. The true population parameter is a fixed, invisible number; it is either inside our specific range (100% chance) or it isn’t (0% chance). We just don’t know which.

What it actually means: If we repeated this exact A/B test 100 times (B = 100), pulling 100 brand new samples of n = 1,176 users and calculating 100 different sample proportions (p̂) and therefore different confidence intervals, the mathematics guarantee that 95 of those 100 intervals would successfully capture the true population parameter. We trust our single result because we trust the rigorous methodology that produced it.

Here’s an analogy using lottery tickets: Think of the true, unknown population parameter as a fixed, secret winning number. It never moves or changes, and it remains completely invisible to us. When we design an A/B test with a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05), our statistical methodology acts like a printing press that generates a bucket of 100 lottery tickets:

  • Each ticket is a unique confidence interval (a range of values) generated by a single sample.
  • 95 of the tickets are “winners” (their printed range successfully captures the secret winning number).
  • 5 of the tickets are “losers” (due to random sampling noise, their printed range completely misses the secret number).

(We could design our methodology to print 99 winners and only 1 loser if we wanted to be stricter, which would require a much larger sample size or a wider net (confidence interval). But in most real-world situations, we generally accept the 95/5 split as the practical sweet spot.)

In the real world, running a single A/B test means reaching into that bucket and pulling out just one ticket at random. Because the true population parameter is invisible, we can never scratch off the ticket to see if our specific range caught the winning number. Our single ticket is strictly binary: it is either a 100% winner or a 0% loser.

Therefore, saying “there is a 95% chance the true parameter is inside our interval” is technically false. Instead, we say “we have 95% confidence” that our interval captures the true parameter because we know the methodology we used to pull that single ticket succeeds 95 out of 100 times.
