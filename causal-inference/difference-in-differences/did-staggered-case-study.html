Staggered Difference in Differences — when everyone starts at different times

October 11, 2026

Methods case study · synthetic data, planted effects per start month (−4 / −3 / −2 kg), true overall effect −3.0 kg — the answer key exists, so everything is checkable. Assumes you know plain difference-in-differences.

The setup

100 patients. A weight-loss drug — but nobody designed an experiment. Some patients started it in January, some in February, some in March, and 40 never took it at all. We can see everyone's weight, every month. Question: did the drug work?

This is the situation observational data usually comes in: treatment happened, but on nobody's schedule but reality's.

Why plain DiD gets confused

In the classic setup there's one "before" and one "after." Here, "after" means January for some patients, February for others, March for the rest. If you lump everyone together and compare "before vs after," you're averaging people whose treatment started months apart — and worse, at any given calendar month, some of your "control" patients already started the drug months ago. Their weight already reflects the treatment, so they stop being a clean stand-in for "what would have happened."

The fix, in one sentence

Line everyone up on "months since they started" instead of calendar month [event time]; do one small, honest DiD per start-month group against the never-treated patients [cohort-specific DiD]; then average the answers [aggregation].

That's the whole machinery. Everything below is just doing it carefully.

The trap: already-treated patients as controls

Watch what happens with one lazy shortcut. For the February group, suppose we use the January patients as the "control" instead of the never-treated — after all, in February the January group isn't "starting" anything.

January change over Feb's window (Oct–Jan vs Feb–Mar): 90.25 → 88.0 = −2.25 (they're still losing weight from the drug).
February change: −2.0.
"DiD" = −2.0 − (−2.25) = +0.25 kg — the drug looks like it causes weight gain.

Using already-treated patients as the control subtracts the drug's own effect and can even flip the sign [forbidden comparisons]. This is the mistake the naive one-big-regression makes quietly, inside its machinery.

Instead of one number, estimate the effect month by month relative to each patient's start — month −1 is the reference (zero by construction). Before starting, the estimates should hover near zero (no effect before the drug exists); after starting, they should sit near the true effect:

Pre-start months (−3, −2): 0.19, 0.27 — essentially zero, as they should be. Post-start months: -2.82, -3.04, -3.04, -2.88 — hovering around the true −3.0. [Event-study plot.] One caution from the fine print: flat pre-trends are reassuring, not proof — they can't show you what would have happened after.

Placebo: the method must stay quiet when nothing happened

Take the 40 never-treated patients, randomly label 20 of them "fake treated," and run the whole machine. Repeat 30 times. Average fake effect: -0.06 kg (truth: 0). It finds nothing, as it should. [Placebo / falsification test.]

What about one big regression?

The naive approach — one regression with patient and month adjustments and a single "on the drug" switch [two-way fixed effects, TWFE] — gives -2.96 kg here, close to the honest answer because our planted effects are steady. Don't let that comfort you: inside, it still mixes already-treated patients into its comparisons (the trap above), and when effects grow or fade over time it can distort badly. The cohort-by-cohort version never lets that happen, because its control group is always genuinely untreated.

When even the good version fails

Suppose doctors prescribed the drug to the patients whose weight was spiking — the worst-off got treated first. Then the treated groups were already on a different trajectory than the never-treated, and no estimator, however careful, can fix that: the comparison was broken at assignment. [Selection on the outcome; the parallel-trends assumption fails.] Same if patients started dieting the month before their prescription (the "baseline" is already affected) [anticipation effects]. Staggered DiD fixes the timing problem; it cannot fix a selection problem. Always ask how people ended up treated.

Limitations, honestly

  • You need never-treated (or not-yet-treated) people. If everyone eventually takes the drug, the comparison runs out — you can only study horizons where a valid control group still exists.
  • Keep the comparison window common. Don't compare month 6 (only January starters observed that long) against month 1 (everyone observed) and call the difference "the effect growing" — the mix of patients changed, not necessarily the effect.
  • Synthetic is clean; reality isn't. Real prescribing has messy timing, partial adherence, and spillovers (partners dieting together). The machinery is the same; the vigilance has to be higher.
The 30-second version for your boss.

"We wanted to know if the weight-loss drug worked, but patients started it in different months, so there's no single 'before and after.' We lined everyone up on months since they started instead of calendar month, compared each start-month group against patients who never took it, and averaged the results: about 3 kg of weight loss. The key discipline: never use already-treated patients as the comparison group — their weight already reflects the drug, and doing so can make a working drug look harmful. The one judgment call, as always, is that the groups would have trended together without the drug — which is about why people started it when they did, not something a chart can prove."
