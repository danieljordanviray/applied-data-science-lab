Staggered Difference-in-Differences

October 22, 2026

Author: Daniel Jordan Viray

The motivating question: CRISPR gene-therapy centers are opening region by region, at different times. Did opening a center reduce crisis-related ER visits for sickle cell patients — or would visits have changed anyway?

The problem. The centers didn’t all open on one date, and nobody randomized the order. So there is no single “before” and “after” to compare — and ordinary difference-in-differences assumes there is.

The plan. Do it one wave at a time. For each region that opens, compare its change against regions that haven’t opened yet. Then average the wave answers into one number.

01

The motivating example

Casgevy, the first CRISPR gene therapy (FDA-approved December 2023 for sickle cell disease), is given only at authorized treatment centers — and those centers switched on in waves, region by region [staggered treatment adoption]. Here is a small toy version with four regions. The outcome is crisis-related ER visits per 100 patients per month.

In this toy world, a working center really does cut visits: by 4 a month in Raleigh and 3 a month in Boston. That’s the planted truth. Now pretend you don’t know it, and try to recover it from the numbers below. (Denver opens too late to measure in this example — it will earn its keep as a control group.)

The measurement rule · every comparison below uses it

A program doesn’t work the day it launches, so don’t measure from launch day. For each region whose center opens:

  1. Before = the average of the 3 months before the opening.
  2. Wait = skip the first 3 months after the opening — the program is still taking hold.
  3. After = the average of the 3 months after the wait.
  4. DiD = (After − Before) for the treated region, minus (After − Before) for a control region over the same calendar months.

02

Why we can’t just use ordinary DiD

Ordinary difference-in-differences [canonical 2×2 DiD] needs three things: one treated group, one control group, and one date when treatment starts. Everyone is “before” until that date and “after” from then on. Subtract the control group’s change from the treated group’s change, and the background trend cancels out.

A wave rollout has none of the three. Raleigh starts in January, Boston in February, Denver in August. There is no single before and after — so who is “the control group”?

Force it anyway, and the trap springs. Say you estimate Boston using Raleigh as the control — Raleigh isn’t in Boston’s wave, so it feels safe. But Raleigh’s center was already open for Boston’s whole span, so Raleigh’s numbers already contain its own −4 effect — and subtracting them subtracts a real treatment effect from Boston’s answer, which can shrink it, erase it, or even flip its sign. Comparisons against an already-treated group [forbidden comparisons] are the core thing to avoid: compare each treated group only against holdouts or regions that haven’t been treated yet.

The fix isn’t a fancier formula. It’s to run a separate small DiD for each wave, each time against regions that are still untreated — then combine the answers. The next three sections do exactly that.

Phoenix never gets a center. It is the holdout [never-treated control]: untreated in every month, so it can stand in for “what would have happened anyway” for any wave. Start with Raleigh. Raleigh’s own change is 89.5 − 90.5 = −1.0 — but visits were rising everywhere, so −1.0 is not the program’s effect. Phoenix’s change over the same months shows the background trend:

Raleigh vs Phoenix: (89.5 − 90.5) − (91.5 − 88.5) = (−1.0) − (+3.0) = −4.0 visits

Exactly the planted truth. Same move for Boston, whose windows shift one month later (Before Nov–Jan, After May–Jul):

Boston vs Phoenix: (87.0 − 87.0) − (92.0 − 89.0) = 0.0 − (+3.0) = −3.0 visits

Notice Boston’s raw change is exactly 0. With no control group you’d say its center did nothing. The control group is what turns “no change” into “−3.”

04

The other option: regions that haven’t been treated yet

A control doesn’t have to stay untreated forever. It only has to be untreated during the months you’re comparing [not-yet-treated controls]. Denver’s center opens in August. Raleigh’s window ends in June and Boston’s in July — so for both waves, Denver is still untreated the whole time. Clean. Raleigh again, now against Denver:

Raleigh vs Denver: (89.5 − 90.5) − (96.8 − 94.4) = (−1.0) − (+2.4) = −3.4 visits
Boston vs Denver: (87.0 − 87.0) − (97.2 − 94.8) = 0.0 − (+2.4) = −2.4 visits

Slightly different answers than the holdout gave (−3.4 instead of −4.0). That’s because Denver’s background drift (+2.4) isn’t identical to Phoenix’s (+3.0). Two honest rulers, slightly different readings — normal. One condition matters: the window has to end before the control’s own center opens. If Denver opened in June instead of August, it couldn’t serve as Raleigh’s control at all.

05

Pooling the answers into one number

Each wave now has two scores — one per control group. You don’t have to pick one. Average them, giving each control group a vote weighted by how many patients it has. Phoenix has 40 patients, Denver 20:

Raleigh: (40 × −4.0 + 20 × −3.4) / (40 + 20) = (−160 − 68) / 60 = −3.8 visits
Boston:  (40 × −3.0 + 20 × −2.4) / (40 + 20) = (−120 − 48) / 60 = −2.8 visits

That’s all pooling means: a weighted average, bigger groups counting more. (Merge Phoenix and Denver into one 60-patient control group first, then do a single subtraction — you get exactly −3.8 and −2.8 again. Same math, two ways to see it.)

The headline number is the same move one level up: average the wave answers, weighted by treated patients. Raleigh and Boston have 20 patients each, so it’s a plain average:

(−3.8 + −2.8) / 2 = −3.3 visits

That single number is the estimated causal effect of the program [average treatment effect on the treated, ATT]: opening a center caused about 3.3 fewer ER visits per 100 patients per month. The whole recipe behind it — one clean DiD per wave, then a weighted average — is a published method, not an improvisation [Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021) group-time ATT estimator]. The planted truth was −3.5 on average (−4 and −3). The small miss is Denver’s slightly different drift leaking into both waves’ scores — real estimates always wobble around the truth like this.

Denver could be measured too — just not with this table, since its After window falls past August. With data covering those months, Denver would be scored against the holdout only (by then Phoenix is the one clean control left) and pooled in with Raleigh and Boston, weighted by its 20 patients, exactly like the waves above.

07

One caveat: anticipation

An anticipation effect [anticipation effect] is when the outcome changes before treatment officially starts, because people know it’s coming and change their behavior in advance.

Say, for example, the Raleigh center opens in January. Anticipation would mean ER visits among eligible patients shift in, say, December, even though no one has been treated yet. A few ways that could happen:

  • Patients delay care or defer procedures to stay stable and eligible, or because they expect to be fixed soon, so ER visits might drop early.
  • Patients get sicker or are pushed into the ER during pre-treatment workups, screening, or while being bridged onto therapy, so visits might rise early.
  • Clinicians change management of patients in the pipeline (for example, adjusting medications or stopping an existing therapy ahead of infusion), which can cause flare-ups.
  • Patients move or switch providers to get access to a site that will have the therapy sooner.

Let’s go with the example where ER visits dip in December.

A brief example — Raleigh again, with exactly one change: December dips from 91.0 to 88.0.

Nothing about the program changed; the After months are identical. But the Before average drops from 90.5 to 89.5, so the DiD reads −3.0 instead of the true −4.0 — a quarter of the effect is hiding in the baseline.

In theory, you account for it by cleaning the baseline: drop the last month or two before each opening from the Before window, or shift the whole window earlier, so “before” means before anyone could react to the news.

08

Assumptions: what has to be true

Parallel trends [parallel trends assumption] — without the program, treated and control regions would have kept moving in parallel. Check: plot the months before any opening; the lines should already be rising together.

No anticipation [no anticipation] — nobody changes behavior before their own center opens. Check: look for a dip or jump in the last months before each opening — section 07 shows what the dip looks like.

No spillovers [no interference · Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA)] — one region’s center doesn’t change another region’s visits. Check: watch control regions bordering a new center; if their visits start falling too, patients are probably crossing over.

Stable measurement [stable composition] — the patients counted, and the way visits are coded, stay the same across regions and months. Check: track patient counts and coding rules over time; a sudden change in either can fake an effect.

09

Limitations: what the number doesn’t tell you

  • The headline is an average: Raleigh’s −4 and Boston’s −3 blend into −3.3, a number that describes no single region exactly.
  • Denver is never measured here, so the estimate says nothing about late-opening regions — their effects could be larger or smaller.
  • Parallel trends can be probed but never proven; if a control region would have drifted differently anyway, the answer is off by that amount.
  • An open center is not a treated patient: the number measures the effect of access [intention-to-treat], not of receiving the therapy itself.
  • The toy numbers are smooth by construction; real claims counts bounce around, so a real estimate needs an uncertainty range, not just a point.
  • Only the first measured months count — effects years later could grow, shrink, or fade.

Every number above comes from the toy table in section 01 and can be checked with a calculator. The formal version of “one DiD per wave, then average” is the group-time ATT of Callaway & Sant’Anna [Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021) group-time ATT estimator] — the place to look if you go on to implement this for real.
